# Layout Critique

This skill channels one question: does the slide read? Not whether it's pretty, not whether it sells - whether a viewer's eye lands in the right place, follows the right path, and walks away with the one thing the slide is for.

The principles come from Massimo Vignelli's design discipline - grid, restraint, hierarchy through contrast rather than noise. They're stripped down to the parts that are universal. This skill does not impose Vignelli's own palette or typeface; type and color come from the project's own brand kit or design tokens if one exists, otherwise a neutral accessible default (stated as an assumption, not invented as a brand). What carries over is the *judgment*, not the Swiss-modernist look.

**Pairing:** sales-lens-review asks whether a rep can sell with this. This asks whether anyone can read it. Different lens, same review pass - they're meant to run back to back on a deck. Run layout-critique first (does it read), then sales-lens-review (does it sell) - a slide that doesn't read rarely sells either, so fixing hierarchy first makes the sales pass more useful.

---

## The core conviction

Strength comes from contrast and restraint, never from loudness. A slide gets its power from one big thing against a lot of quiet - not from filling every corner. The creed underneath all of it: *if you can see the layout, it's a bad layout.* When the structure is doing its job, the viewer notices the content, not the boxes and dividers holding it up.

---

## The checklist

Run a slide (or every slide in a deck) against these. Each is a pass/fail question with the fix attached.

**1. Two type sizes, fighting?**
A slide should have a clear two-level type hierarchy - a takeaway and everything else. If there are four or five competing sizes, the eye doesn't know what's most important. Fix: collapse to two sizes (heading and body), and use weight (bold vs regular) for any further distinction rather than a third size.

**2. Is text flush left?**
Flush left is the readable default. Centered text only earns its place for a title slide, a quote, or something lapidary. Justified text is contrived and opens ugly rivers of whitespace - avoid it. Fix: set body and bullets flush left.

**3. Is white space doing work?**
White space is the protagonist, not wasted space. A crammed slide reads as anxious; an open one reads as confident. The instinct to fill the slide because it "looks empty" is almost always wrong. Fix: cut content before shrinking margins. If it's still crammed, the slide is doing two jobs - split it.

**4. Is there one dominant element?**
Scan the slide for half a second. Does one thing grab the eye, or does everything compete at the same weight? A slide needs a clear entry point - the biggest, boldest, or most isolated element. Fix: pick the one thing that matters and make it visibly dominant; demote the rest.

**5. Can you see the layout?**
Look for decorative chartjunk - drop shadows, gradient fills, boxes around boxes, divider lines that don't separate anything, icons that decorate rather than inform. Every one of those is the layout showing through. Fix: delete anything that isn't carrying meaning. If removing it loses nothing, it was noise.

**6. Is color identifying or decorating?**
Color should mean something - this is the highlighted number, this is the warning, this is our brand. Color sprinkled for variety just adds noise and competes with the one place color should be pulling the eye. Fix: reserve the accent for the thing that matters; let everything else stay neutral. (Brand colors come from the project's own brand kit or design tokens if one exists, otherwise a neutral accessible default - say so rather than inventing a brand.)

**7. Does the eye have a path?**
A deck is a sequence, not a pile. Across slides, does the structure stay consistent so the viewer isn't relearning the layout each time? Within a slide, does the eye move in a deliberate order - takeaway, then support, then detail? Fix: hold the grid steady slide to slide; lead with the takeaway, not the buildup.

---

## How to deliver feedback

Be concrete and specific. "This slide is busy" is useless; "you've got four type sizes and three boxes competing - kill the inner box and drop the subhead to body weight" is usable. Point at the specific element and name the fix.

Go slide by slide for a deck, but lead with the one or two issues that show up everywhere - a consistent problem across the deck matters more than a one-off. If a slide passes all seven, say so plainly and move on; don't manufacture problems to seem rigorous.

When the verdict is "this is trying to do two things at once" - which is the most common real problem - say it directly. Most cluttered slides aren't a styling problem, they're a slide that should be two slides.
